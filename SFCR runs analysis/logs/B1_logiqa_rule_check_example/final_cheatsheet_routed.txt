**LogiQA Logical Reading Comprehension Cheat Sheet**

---

### 1. Core Reasoning Strategies

- **Identify Logical Relationships:**  
  - Look for cause-effect, condition-consequence, necessity, and sufficiency.  
  - Translate conditional statements into “if-then” or contrapositives to clarify logic.  
  - Check whether premises support or contradict conclusions.

- **Distinguish Premises, Conclusions, and Assumptions:**  
  - Premises provide evidence or facts.  
  - Conclusions are claims drawn from premises.  
  - Assumptions are unstated but necessary for the argument to hold.

- **Spot Logical Flaws and Fallacies:**  
  - Beware of *appeal to ignorance* (assuming truth from lack of disproof).  
  - Watch for *false dilemmas* (ignoring alternatives).  
  - Identify *confusing correlation with causation*.  
  - Detect *ambiguity* or *equivocation* in terms.

- **Use Contradiction and Counterexamples:**  
  - Test statements by assuming the opposite to check consistency.  
  - Consider alternative explanations or confounding factors.

- **Analyze Quantifiers and Scope Carefully:**  
  - Note words like “all,” “some,” “only,” “must,” “can,” “usually.”  
  - Understand whether statements are universal, particular, or probabilistic.

- **Apply Deductive Constraints in Logic Puzzles:**  
  - Use given conditions to eliminate impossible assignments.  
  - Track dependencies and exclusivity (e.g., “only one,” “if-then,” “not both”).  
  - Use adjacency and order relations carefully.

- **Evaluate Relevance and Strength of Evidence:**  
  - Determine which facts directly support or weaken a conclusion.  
  - Distinguish between evidence that questions validity vs. practical feasibility.

- **Consider Temporal and Contextual Factors:**  
  - Recognize how time affects judgments (e.g., subjective opinions becoming objective over time).  
  - Understand context that might influence interpretation (e.g., cultural, scientific).

---

### 2. Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Confusing Cause and Effect:**  
  - Don’t assume correlation implies causation without explicit support.

- **Ignoring Alternative Explanations:**  
  - Avoid accepting a single cause when multiple plausible causes exist.

- **Overgeneralizing or Oversimplifying:**  
  - Beware of conclusions that ignore exceptions or complexity (e.g., multiple religions, diverse populations).

- **Misreading Conditional Statements:**  
  - Don’t confuse “if” with “only if” or ignore the contrapositive.

- **Assuming Truth of Premises Without Question:**  
  - Always check if premises are supported or if assumptions are needed.

- **Falling for False Dilemmas:**  
  - Don’t accept arguments that limit options artificially.

- **Neglecting the Role of Assumptions:**  
  - Missing key assumptions can invalidate reasoning.

- **Ignoring the Difference Between Absolute and Relative Measures:**  
  - For example, total number vs. rate or proportion.

- **Confusing Logical Equivalence with Similar Wording:**  
  - Similar phrasing doesn’t guarantee identical meaning.

- **Failing to Distinguish Between Evidence and Opinion:**  
  - Recognize when statements are subjective or speculative.

---

### 3. Quick Decision Rules (If X → Do Y)

- **If the argument relies on a cause-effect link → Identify and test the necessary assumptions that connect cause to effect.**

- **If a conclusion seems contradicted by data → Look for missing premises or alternative explanations that reconcile the contradiction.**

- **If multiple premises lead to an absurd conclusion → At least one premise must be false or the reasoning invalid; identify which.**

- **If asked to find the statement “different in meaning” → Compare options for shifts in logical relations, focus, or implied assumptions.**

- **If a question involves “must be true” or “must be assumed” → Select statements that are necessary conditions for the argument’s validity.**

- **If a reasoning puzzle involves assignments or ordering → Use process of elimination and apply all given constraints step-by-step.**

- **If an argument uses absence of disproof as proof → Recognize this as a logical fallacy (appeal to ignorance).**

- **If an argument ignores alternative possibilities or pluralities → Identify this as a false dilemma or oversimplification.**

- **If the question asks for the “weakest” or “least relevant” challenge → Choose options that do not directly undermine the core logic or evidence.**

- **If the argument’s strength depends on practical adoption or acceptance → Assess economic, social, or psychological feasibility as key factors.**

- **If the question involves statistical or experimental claims → Check for confounding variables, control conditions, and sample representativeness.**

- **If the argument compares groups or conditions → Ensure comparability (e.g., same rank, mileage, or context) before inferring causality.**
