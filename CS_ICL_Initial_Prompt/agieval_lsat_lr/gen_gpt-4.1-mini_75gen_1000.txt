**Cheat Sheet for Tackling Difficult Logical Reasoning Questions**

---

### 1. **Assumption Questions (Necessary Assumptions)**  
- **Key:** Identify what must be true for the argument’s conclusion to hold.  
- **Strategy:**  
  - Find the gap between premises and conclusion.  
  - Ask: *What is the unstated premise that bridges this gap?*  
  - Use the negation test: if negating the choice destroys the argument, it’s necessary.  
- **Common traps:**  
  - Confusing sufficient assumptions with necessary ones.  
  - Choosing statements that are true but not required for the argument.  
- **Example:**  
  - Editorial on school calendar assumes that removing long breaks benefits children (E).  
  - Psychologist on insomnia assumes cases treated are representative of all insomnia (E).  
  - Archaeologist assumes only archaeological considerations matter (A).  
  - Consumer advocate assumes convenience outweighs other factors for some consumers (A).  
  - Gardening books assume explaining hot vs. cold composting is part of basics (C).  
  - Economist assumes large corporations promote communal ties (D).  
  - Editorialist on tree rights assumes no obligation to entities other than trees (D).  
  - Lawyer on jury instructions assumes simple language can adequately describe jurors’ role (C, E).  

---

### 2. **Flawed Reasoning / Logical Fallacies**  
- **Common flawed patterns:**  
  - **Confusing necessary and sufficient conditions:**  
    - E.g., "If A then B" does not imply "If not B then not A."  
    - Parallel example: Calcium necessary for healthy bones → concluding no healthy bones means no calcium (B).  
  - **Circular reasoning:**  
    - The conclusion is assumed in the premise.  
    - E.g., cereal health claims justified by health-conscious consumers buying it because they are health-conscious (B).  
  - **Confusing correlation with causation / Reverse causality:**  
    - E.g., discipline causes misbehavior vs. misbehavior causes discipline (A).  
  - **Overgeneralization from limited examples:**  
    - E.g., concluding all foods will be reported healthful based on two examples (C).  
  - **Assuming motivation equals outcome:**  
    - E.g., assuming technology motivated by personal gain cannot benefit society (D).  
  - **Assuming group majority overlaps:**  
    - E.g., most mechanics have experience, most experienced understand circuits → most mechanics understand circuits (E).  
  - **Attacking the person instead of the argument (ad hominem):**  
    - E.g., rejecting proposal because proposer acted inconsistently in past (E).  

---

### 3. **Strengthen / Weaken Questions**  
- **Strengthen:**  
  - Look for evidence that supports the causal link or key assumption.  
  - Eliminate alternative explanations.  
  - Provide direct support for the argument’s main claim.  
- **Weaken:**  
  - Introduce alternative causes or explanations.  
  - Show the key assumption is false or unsupported.  
  - Provide counterexamples or contradictory evidence.  
- **Examples:**  
  - Magnet experiment weakened by placebo effect (A).  
  - Microbiologist strengthened by bacteria in sludge without heavy metals lacking resistances (B).  
  - Psychologist strengthened by supervisors not influenced by noticed flattery (E).  
  - City jury fairness weakened by expert jurors’ bias (B).  
  - Local resident’s algae-fish deaths argument weakened by common cause possibility (D).  

---

### 4. **Parallel Reasoning**  
- **Match the logical structure, not the topic.**  
- **Look for:**  
  - Similar argument form (e.g., necessary condition confusion, circular reasoning, causal inference).  
  - Similar flaw or pattern of reasoning.  
- **Examples:**  
  - Calcium and cake flour necessary condition confusion (B).  
  - Mechanics and snow-removal companies majority overlap error (E).  
  - Cereal health claims and card game intellectual challenge circular reasoning (B).  

---

### 5. **Inference / Must Be True Questions**  
- **Only choose what must be true based on the passage.**  
- **Avoid:**  
  - Overgeneralizing or assuming facts not stated.  
  - Introducing new information or speculation.  
- **Examples:**  
  - Museum prints must include all permanent collection works (C).  
  - Florida panthers need larger habitat to be self-sustaining (C).  
  - Government’s financial predicament depends on computer modeling investment (B).  

---

### 6. **Principle Questions**  
- **Identify the general rule that underlies the argument.**  
- **Match the principle that best captures the reasoning pattern.**  
- **Example:**  
  - Chairperson’s caution about political instability → principle that corporations should be cautious expanding into unstable countries (D).  
  - Artistic period understanding requires knowledge of influential earlier periods → principle about understanding intellectual antecedents (E).  

---

### 7. **Role of a Statement / Function Questions**  
- **Determine how a particular statement functions in the argument:** premise, conclusion, assumption, objection, illustration, etc.  
- **Example:**  
  - Zooplankton feeding on phytoplankton shows indirect effect of temperature on larger sea animals (C).  
  - Statement about recent scientific research’s esoteric language establishes relevance of analogy to Newton’s Principia (D).  
  - Assertion about strength and coordination is one reason for supporting athletics, to which another reason is added (A).  

---

### 8. **Conditional Reasoning and Contrapositives**  
- **Remember:**  
  - "If P then Q" → contrapositive "If not Q then not P" is logically equivalent.  
  - Converse "If Q then P" is not necessarily true.  
- **Use this to analyze must-be-true and assumption questions.**  
- **Example:**  
  - Keyboarding skills → computer use → word processing → being able to write essays implies some keyboarding skills (C).  

---

### 9. **Common Logical Moves to Watch For**  
- **Assuming exclusivity:**  
  - E.g., only brothers could have given the gift → ignoring other possibilities (D).  
- **Assuming uniformity:**  
  - E.g., all ball lightning has the same cause (E).  
- **Assuming representativeness:**  
  - E.g., cases treated by psychologists represent all cases (E).  
- **Assuming causation from correlation:**  
  - E.g., discipline causes misbehavior without considering reverse causation (A).  
- **Assuming rights imply obligations or vice versa:**  
  - E.g., having a right means having an obligation (B).  

---

### 10. **Test-Taking Tips for Difficult Questions**  
- **Read carefully:** Identify conclusion and premises clearly.  
- **Look for hidden assumptions:** What must be true for the argument to work?  
- **Use process of elimination:** Remove choices that are irrelevant, too strong, or unrelated.  
- **Apply negation test for assumption questions:** Negate the choice; if argument falls apart, it’s necessary.  
- **For weaken questions:** Find alternative explanations or challenge key assumptions.  
- **For strengthen questions:** Find evidence that supports the causal link or assumption.  
- **For parallel reasoning:** Focus on logical form, not content.  
- **For inference:** Only choose what is directly supported by the passage.
