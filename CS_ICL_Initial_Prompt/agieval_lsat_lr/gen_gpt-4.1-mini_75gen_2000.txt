**Cheat Sheet for Tackling Difficult Logical Reasoning Questions**

---

### 1. **Must-Be-True / Inference Questions**

- **Key challenge:** Avoid overgeneralizing or assuming more than what is stated.
- **Strategy:**
  - Identify exactly what is stated.
  - Check if the option is *necessarily* true given the premises.
  - Watch for qualifiers like "always," "generally," or "most"—these often make an option too strong.
  - Beware of confusing correlation with causation.
  - Example flaw to avoid: Assuming *all* ineffective whipping results in a thick, velvety substance when only blender whipping is discussed.

---

### 2. **Flaw Questions (Common Flaws to Recognize)**

- **Ignoring alternative explanations:**  
  - Example: Correlation between algae and fish deaths may be due to a third factor causing both.
- **Assuming past conditions apply unchanged to present:**  
  - Example: Assuming "latest safety technology" 30 years ago is equivalent to today's.
- **Confusing absence of evidence with evidence of absence:**  
  - Not having proof that something is false does not prove it true.
- **Appeal to authority/reputation instead of evidence:**  
  - Trusting a study because the author is reputable, ignoring methodological flaws.
- **Overgeneralization from small or unrepresentative samples:**  
  - Drawing broad conclusions from 11 volunteers or only older subjects.
- **Confusing cause and effect:**  
  - Assuming regulation caused lower exposure without ruling out other factors.
- **Assuming right to do something implies obligation to do it:**  
  - Having a right to publish ≠ obligation to publish.
- **Assuming correlation implies causation:**  
  - Just because two things occur together does not mean one causes the other.

---

### 3. **Assumption Questions**

- **Look for the missing link that must be true for the conclusion to hold.**
- **Common assumption types:**
  - **Equivalence assumptions:**  
    - E.g., Explaining basics of composting *must* include explaining hot vs. cold composting.
  - **Exclusivity or appropriateness assumptions:**  
    - E.g., Previous president acted appropriately, so current president’s similar action is justified.
  - **Causal assumptions:**  
    - E.g., Refusing competitor’s coupons when it costs nothing and satisfies customers must be motivated solely by desire to hurt competitor.
  - **Category membership assumptions:**  
    - E.g., Anger is a visceral emotion (to apply a general claim about visceral emotions to anger).
  - **Linking right and obligation:**  
    - E.g., Having a right to do something entails having an obligation to do it.

---

### 4. **Strengthen/Weaken Questions**

- **To strengthen:**  
  - Provide evidence that supports the causal link or key assumption.
  - Show that the criticized factor actually has the effect claimed.
  - Example: Showing bacteria in sludge without heavy metals are not antibiotic resistant strengthens the claim that heavy metals promote resistance.
- **To weaken:**  
  - Provide alternative explanations.
  - Show that the key assumption is false or incomplete.
  - Example: Showing managers donate with owners’ tacit consent weakens the stealing analogy.

---

### 5. **Parallel Reasoning / Pattern Matching**

- Identify the logical form of the argument:
  - Conditional reasoning (If P then Q; Not Q; therefore Not P) — watch for hidden assumptions.
  - Reasoning from "usually does X that can only be done at Y" to "sometimes must be at Y."
  - Analogies based on precedent or similarity require assuming the precedent was appropriate.
- Match the pattern exactly, including the strength of the conclusion (some vs. all, sometimes vs. always).

---

### 6. **Evaluating Causal Arguments**

- Check for overlooked alternative causes or confounding factors.
- Beware of adding percentages without considering overlap.
- Example: 30% have drainage problems + 30% have structural defects ≠ 60% total if some houses have both.

---

### 7. **Evaluating Arguments Involving Scientific or Technical Claims**

- Distinguish between:
  - Changes in knowledge vs. changes in reality.
  - Correlation vs. causation.
  - Sample representativeness.
- Watch for assumptions about the uniqueness of biomarkers or the exclusivity of biological origins.

---

### 8. **Dialogue / Disagreement Questions**

- Identify the exact point of disagreement, not just related issues.
- Often the disagreement is about:
  - Whether a claim is true or false.
  - Whether a practice is valuable or not.
  - Whether a condition is necessary or sufficient.
- Avoid choosing options that are agreed upon or irrelevant.

---

### 9. **Logical Connectors and Conditional Reasoning**

- Understand the difference between:
  - Necessary vs. sufficient conditions.
  - If-then statements vs. biconditionals.
- Avoid assuming that failure of the consequent proves failure of the antecedent without ruling out other causes.

---

### 10. **Common Logical Reasoning Pitfalls**

- **Equivocation:** Using a term in two different senses.
- **Circular reasoning:** Assuming what you are trying to prove.
- **Straw man:** Misrepresenting the opponent’s argument.
- **Ad hominem:** Attacking the person instead of the argument.
- **False dichotomy:** Presenting two options as the only possibilities.

---

### 11. **Key Tips for Difficult Questions**

- **Break down complex arguments into premises and conclusions.**
- **Identify what is explicitly stated vs. what is assumed or implied.**
- **Look for hidden assumptions that connect premises to conclusions.**
- **Be wary of strong language in answer choices ("always," "never," "only").**
- **For must-be-true questions, test the answer choice against the stimulus to see if it must hold.**
- **For flaw questions, identify the gap or error in reasoning.**
- **For strengthen/weaken, focus on the causal link or key assumption.**
- **For parallel reasoning, diagram the argument’s logical form before matching.**
