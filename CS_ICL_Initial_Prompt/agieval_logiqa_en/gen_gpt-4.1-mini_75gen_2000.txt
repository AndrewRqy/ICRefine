**Cheat Sheet for Difficult Logical Reasoning Problems**

---

### 1. **Identifying Assumptions in Arguments**

- **Assumption** = an unstated premise that must be true for the argument to hold.
- To find assumptions:
  - Identify the conclusion.
  - Identify the premises.
  - Ask: What must be true for the premises to support the conclusion?
- **Example:**  
  - If argument compares presence/absence of a practice (e.g., reading classics) to cultural vitality, assumption is that practice (e.g., primary school reading) is a valid symbol of cultural maintenance.

---

### 2. **Explaining Seemingly Contradictory Attitudes**

- When an expert’s attitude seems contradictory (e.g., wanting more carriers despite US concerns), look for statements that explain strategic or political reasons, such as:
  - External countries stirring up threat theories.
  - The expert’s attitude is a response to external pressure or narrative.
- Avoid options that only state facts without explaining motivation.

---

### 3. **Personnel Allocation Logic Problems**

- **Stepwise approach:**
  1. List all constraints clearly.
  2. Check each option against all constraints.
  3. Use contrapositive logic for conditional constraints (e.g., If X in sales → W in production means if W not in production → X not in sales).
  4. Pay attention to "must be in same/different group" and "cannot be in same group" conditions.
- **Common pitfalls:**
  - Overlooking conditional constraints.
  - Ignoring group size requirements.

---

### 4. **Evaluating Cause-Effect Arguments (e.g., Aluminum and Behavior Disorder)**

- Key assumption: The factor identified (e.g., aluminum) is the **cause**, not the effect.
- If the factor is a result, removing it won’t treat the disorder.
- Safety or dosage assumptions are not necessary for the logical validity of the argument.

---

### 5. **Weakening Arguments by Clarifying Levels of Strategy**

- When two views seem contradictory (e.g., quick war vs. protracted war), check if they refer to different levels:
  - Tactics (short-term, battle-level)
  - Strategy (long-term, campaign-level)
- Showing that the two views address different levels weakens the claim of inconsistency.

---

### 6. **Historical/Scientific Migration or Origin Arguments**

- When current geography contradicts origin claims, look for historical or geological explanations (e.g., past river connections, glacial melt forming lakes connected to oceans).
- Size or survival rates of species are irrelevant to migration origin.
- Experimental breeding success far away supports viability but not origin.

---

### 7. **Must-Be-True Conditions for Conclusions**

- To conclude "more people dine out because more restaurants open," must assume:
  - New restaurants open only if demand exceeds existing capacity.
- Popularity or total number of restaurants alone is insufficient.
- Look for assumptions linking supply increase to demand increase.

---

### 8. **Patent and Control Arguments**

- When foreign patents are involved, the key concern is **control over resources**.
- The strongest support for loss of control is that foreign patent holders can restrict seed saving and force repeated purchases.
- Economic burden or lack of yield advantage does not directly support control loss.

---

### 9. **Causality in Brain Structure Changes**

- To claim training causes brain changes, assume no pre-existing difference before training.
- If difference existed before training, causality is questionable.
- Uniformity among trainees or absence of other activities is not necessary.

---

### 10. **Appeal to Authority**

- When an argument relies on an expert’s claim to reject a premise, it is an **appeal to authority**.
- No experimental data or direct evidence is presented.
- Recognize this as a reasoning technique.

---

### 11. **Logical Contrapositives in Economic Arguments**

- If "Return of capital cost to reasonable level → control of economic diseases," then contrapositive is:  
  "If diseases not controlled → capital cost not reasonable."
- Use contrapositive to evaluate must-be-true statements.

---

### 12. **Flaws in Using Single Indicators (e.g., GDP per capita)**

- GDP per capita is an average and does not reflect income distribution or living standards.
- Judging overall living standards solely by GDP per capita is unreliable.
- Look for flaws related to ignoring inequality, poverty, or other social factors.

---

### 13. **Hypotheses Underlying Arguments About Civilization**

- When argument hinges on suppressing instinct via foresight, the key hypothesis is:  
  **Greater suppression of instinct → greater tolerance of immediate pain → higher civilization level.**
- Other options about animal behavior or external laws are not necessary assumptions.

---

### 14. **Hypotheses in Complaints About English Proficiency**

- If students admitted meet English entry requirements, the complaint assumes:  
  **English proficiency required for entry = English proficiency required to pass courses.**
- Without this, complaint about English skills is invalid.

---

### 15. **Logic Puzzles with Exactly One True Statement**

- When given multiple statements and told only one is true, check for logical consistency:
  - If statement A implies statement B is true, both cannot be true if only one is allowed.
  - Use this to eliminate impossible options.
- Carefully analyze implications and contradictions.

---

### 16. **Salary Comparison and Assumptions**

- To conclude state-owned airline salaries are too low based on salary differences, assume:  
  **If pilots who stayed moved to private airlines, they would also earn more.**
- This rules out differences due to pilot ability or rank.

---

### 17. **Inferring Inner States from External Signs**

- The key assumption is:  
  **Similar external behaviors imply similar internal experiences.**
- This justifies inferring others’ feelings from one’s own experience.

---

### 18. **Identifying Non-Criticisms in Multiple-Choice**

- When asked which option is NOT a criticism, look for statements that do not question or doubt the original claim.
- Statements that acknowledge benefits or neutral facts are usually NOT criticisms.

---

### 19. **Group Assignment with Multiple Conditions**

- Translate all conditions into inequalities or logical relations.
- Use adjacency and ordering constraints to eliminate impossible assignments.
- Check each candidate assignment against all conditions.

---

### 20. **Evaluating Cost-Benefit Decisions**

- To evaluate if a decision to reduce theft via monitoring is worthwhile, compare:  
  **Losses prevented by theft reduction vs. cost of monitoring and staff.**
- This ratio or comparison is the key evaluation metric.

---

### 21. **Evaluating Survey Data with Overlapping Percentages**

- If percentages sum to more than 100%, likely respondents selected multiple options.
- This explains apparent contradictions in data.

---

### 22. **Reasoning by Example and Generalization**

- When an argument lists examples and then generalizes, the reasoning is inductive.
- Look for options that similarly use examples to support a general conclusion.

---

### 23. **Freedom of Thought and Intellectual Progress**

- To argue freedom of thought alone is insufficient, assume:  
  **Thinkers in free societies lack necessary rules/laws of thinking.**
- This shows freedom without structure cannot guarantee progress.

---

### 24. **Moral Arguments on Genetic Testing**

- Strongest support comes from principles of equality and inherent value of all humans, regardless of sex or defects.
- Practical or social consequences are weaker supports.

---

### 25. **Weakening Ecological Arguments**

- To weaken a claim that species A does not cause decline in species B because A rarely eats B, show indirect effects such as competition for shared food.
- Direct predation is not the only ecological interaction.

---

### 26. **Evaluating Gambler’s Fallacy**

- Recognize that independent events (coin tosses) do not influence each other.
- Past outcomes do not affect future probabilities.

---

### 27. **Assumptions About Influence of Views on Behavior**

- If changing views is claimed to prevent behavior (e.g., suicide), assume:  
  **Views have decisive influence on behavior occurrence.**

---

### 28. **Logical Fallacy: Rejecting Imperfect Solutions**

- Arguments rejecting a method because it is imperfect commit the fallacy of demanding perfection.
- Similar to rejecting police help because they cannot stop all crime.

---

### 29. **Assumptions in Price and Profit Arguments**

- To conclude high prices imply high profits, assume prices reflect profit margins, not just high base prices or market distortions.

---

### 30. **Questioning Generalizations Based on Past Examples**

- To question a conclusion that a pattern will repeat, provide examples where the pattern did not hold or show unique factors in the current case.

---

### 31. **Logical Consistency in Truth-Telling Puzzles**

- Translate statements into logical conditions.
- Use truth tables or logical deduction to find consistent truth assignments.

---

### 32. **Determining Full Assignments from Partial Info**

- Use given assignments and constraints to deduce unique assignments.
- If multiple possibilities remain, the info is insufficient.

---

### 33. **Evaluating Necessity of Free Speech**

- Statements that say free speech alone is insufficient do NOT weaken the conclusion that free speech is essential.
- Statements that deny the government’s function or claim officials already know needs do weaken the conclusion.

---

### 34. **Hypotheses for Safety Conclusions**

- To conclude non-green/non-sprouted potatoes are safe, assume:  
  **Potatoes that are neither green nor sprouted are safe to eat.**

---

### 35. **Economic Hypotheses in Industry Transfer**

- Hypothesis: Developed countries value environment more than developing countries, explaining transfer of polluting industries.

---

### 36. **Questioning Post-Event Economic Predictions**

- To question a predicted recession after an event (e.g., Olympics), provide evidence of lasting benefits or alternative growth drivers.

---

### 37. **Interpreting Survey Data on Parental Support**

- Total support = absolute support + support after persuasion.
- Remaining percentage = parents who do not support.

---

### 38. **Behavioral Ecology Reasoning**

- Animals follow others carrying prey only if prey is social/group-living.
- Solitary prey does not signal more food nearby.

---

### 39. **Determining Non-Red Costume Athletes**

- Use constraints on finishing order and red costume distribution.
- Identify athletes who cannot be red due to position and red distribution rules.

---

### 40. **Evaluating Logical Reasoning in Debates**

- Flaw: Ignoring objective standards beyond subjective opinions leads to "invincible" stalemate.
- Recognize missing external criteria for truth.

---

### 41. **Supporting Expert Opinions on Experiment Rigor**

- Show that human exposure differs from experimental conditions (e.g., distance, frequency).
- Plant experiments do not directly translate to human health effects.

---

### 42. **Evaluating Risk Arguments**

- To evaluate if hospital visits increase death risk for cancer patients, ask:  
  **What percentage would die of cancer anyway without medical accidents?**

---

### 43. **Logical OR Statements from Conditional Outcomes**

- If two mutually exclusive actions lead to two different outcomes, then at least one outcome must occur (logical OR).

---

### 44. **Market Behavior and Consumer Knowledge**

- Sensible consumers buy only if price advantage exists.
- If price advantage does not exist, sensible consumers do not buy abroad.
- Buyers abroad despite no advantage lack market knowledge.

---

### 45. **Maximizing Group Assignments Under Constraints**

- Translate conditions into inequalities.
- Use adjacency and exclusivity to find maximum group size.

---

### 46. **Corollaries from Comparative Data**

- Avoid overgeneralization.
- Corollaries must be directly supported by data, not assumptions.

---

### 47. **Evaluating Critics’ Rebuttals**

- If original claim is about possibility ("some farms can succeed"), critics’ demand for representativeness is irrelevant.
- Distinguish between showing possibility vs. generalization.

---

### 48. **Raising Doubts About Expert Opinions**

- Introduce alternative explanations (e.g., environmental changes) to challenge single-cause claims.

---

### 49. **Logical Fallacies: Gambler’s Fallacy**

- Recognize independence of events.
- Past outcomes do not affect future probabilities.

---

### 50. **Logical Consistency in Truth-Telling**

- Translate statements into logical conditions.
- Use contradiction and consistency to find who lies or tells truth.

---

**Use this cheat sheet to focus on the key logical structures, assumptions, and reasoning patterns that are challenging. Practice applying these principles to similar problems for mastery.**
