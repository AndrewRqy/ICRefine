**LogiQA (Logical Reading Comprehension) Cheat Sheet**

---

### 1. Core Reasoning Strategies

- **Identify Logical Structure:**  
  Break down arguments into premises and conclusions. Understand whether the reasoning is deductive, inductive, or causal.

- **Check Assumptions:**  
  Determine what unstated assumptions the argument relies on. Ask: *What must be true for this conclusion to hold?*

- **Evaluate Cause and Effect Carefully:**  
  Avoid confusing correlation with causation. Verify if the argument mistakenly affirms the consequent or denies the antecedent.

- **Distinguish Between Evidence and Conclusion:**  
  Identify which statements serve as evidence (premises) and which are conclusions. Sometimes premises support intermediate conclusions.

- **Consider Alternative Explanations:**  
  When asked to weaken or explain inconsistencies, propose plausible alternative causes or factors that challenge the original reasoning.

- **Use Contraposition and Logical Equivalence Correctly:**  
  Recognize valid logical forms (e.g., modus ponens, modus tollens) and avoid invalid inferences like affirming the consequent.

- **Apply Principle of Independence in Probability:**  
  Past independent events do not affect future probabilities (e.g., coin tosses).

- **Analyze Quantifiers and Universal Statements:**  
  Pay attention to words like *all*, *none*, *some*, *only*, and how they affect the scope of conclusions.

- **Test Hypotheticals and Conditionals:**  
  For “if-then” statements, consider what happens if the antecedent or consequent is true or false.

- **Distinguish Between Necessary and Sufficient Conditions:**  
  Identify whether a premise is required (necessary) or enough (sufficient) for the conclusion.

- **Look for Contradictions or Logical Inconsistencies:**  
  When multiple statements are given, check which can be true simultaneously and which contradict.

- **Infer from Comparative Data:**  
  When statistics or comparisons are given, interpret what they imply logically (e.g., relative shares, growth rates).

- **Recognize Logical Fallacies:**  
  Examples include appeal to ignorance, affirming the consequent, false cause, slippery slope, and hasty generalization.

---

### 2. Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Assuming Past Events Affect Independent Future Events:**  
  E.g., believing coin toss outcomes influence each other.

- **Confusing Correlation with Causation:**  
  Just because two things occur together doesn’t mean one causes the other.

- **Overgeneralizing from Limited Data:**  
  Drawing universal conclusions from insufficient or biased samples.

- **Ignoring Alternative Explanations:**  
  Accepting one explanation without considering others that fit the facts.

- **Misinterpreting Quantifiers:**  
  Treating “some” as “all” or “no” as “some” leads to wrong conclusions.

- **Affirming the Consequent or Denying the Antecedent:**  
  Invalid logical moves that reverse cause-effect relationships.

- **Failing to Identify the Main Conclusion:**  
  Confusing background information or examples with the argument’s core claim.

- **Assuming Intent or Motivation Without Evidence:**  
  Inferring reasons behind actions without explicit support.

- **Ignoring Conditions or Restrictions in Statements:**  
  Overlooking qualifications like “only if,” “must,” or “cannot.”

- **Taking Statistical Data at Face Value Without Context:**  
  Not questioning sample bias, measurement accuracy, or interpretation.

- **Assuming All Relevant Information is Provided:**  
  Sometimes key information is missing or implied; be cautious.

---

### 3. Quick Decision Rules (If X → Do Y)

- **If the argument relies on a causal claim → Check for alternative causes or reverse causality.**

- **If a universal statement is given (e.g., “all A are B”) and a negative instance is observed (not B) → Conclude the subject is not A.**

- **If asked to weaken an argument → Identify and challenge key assumptions or provide counterexamples.**

- **If asked to strengthen/support an argument → Provide evidence that eliminates alternative explanations or confirms assumptions.**

- **If statistical data contradicts expectations → Look for sampling bias, measurement error, or external factors affecting data.**

- **If premises conflict and only one can be true → Test each premise’s truth individually to find the consistent scenario.**

- **If an argument uses absence of disproof as proof (appeal to ignorance) → Recognize this as a logical fallacy.**

- **If conditional statements are involved → Use modus ponens (If P then Q; P is true; therefore Q) and modus tollens (If P then Q; Q is false; therefore P is false) correctly.**

- **If multiple groups or categories are involved with membership conditions → Use process of elimination and cross-reference conditions to assign correctly.**

- **If the argument depends on definitions or classifications → Verify that the definitions are consistently applied and assumptions about categories are explicit.**

- **If the argument involves probability or chance → Remember that independent events do not influence each other’s outcomes.**

- **If the argument involves trade-offs (e.g., cost vs. profit) → Identify conflicting interests and infer conditions under which conflicts arise or are resolved.**
