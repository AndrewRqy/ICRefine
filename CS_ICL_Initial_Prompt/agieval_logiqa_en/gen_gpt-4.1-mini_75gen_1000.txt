**Logical Reasoning & Critical Thinking Cheat Sheet**

---

### 1. **Conditional Reasoning & Contrapositives**

- **If A then B**  
  - Contrapositive: If not B then not A (valid)  
  - Denying the antecedent or affirming the consequent is invalid.

- When given conditions like:  
  - "If codonopsis, then atractylodes"  
  - "If Shouwu, then atractylodes"  
  - And exclusivity constraints (e.g., ginseng and atractylodes macrocephala cannot coexist), analyze for contradictions by chaining conditions and contrapositives.

- **Key:**  
  - Identify necessary conditions and exclusivity.  
  - Look for forced presence/absence of elements.  
  - If contradictions arise, conclude which element cannot be present.

---

### 2. **Strengthening & Weakening Arguments**

- To **strengthen** a necessity claim (e.g., "X must do Y"), find premises that make Y **necessary** for X’s success or goal.  
  - Example: "If you do not take English tutoring, you cannot pass" → makes tutoring necessary.

- To **weaken** an argument:  
  - Challenge key assumptions (e.g., that a condition applies to the subject).  
  - Provide counterexamples or alternative explanations.  
  - Show that the premise does not guarantee the conclusion.

- **Common patterns:**  
  - Strengthen necessity by showing no alternative.  
  - Weaken by showing exceptions or alternative causes.

---

### 3. **Deductive Reasoning with Multiple Constraints**

- When selecting or assigning entities under multiple rules:  
  - List all constraints clearly.  
  - Use process of elimination and logical deduction.  
  - Pay attention to "if-then" conditions and exclusivity.  
  - Remember "at least one" or "at most" conditions.

- **Example:**  
  - "If E selected, then A and B selected."  
  - "At least one Dingding selected."  
  - "Max 2 people selected."  
  - Use these to deduce who must or must not be selected.

---

### 4. **Logical Consistency in Statements**

- When multiple statements are given and only one is true:  
  - Test each statement as true, check if others become false.  
  - The scenario where exactly one statement is true is the solution.

- When statements contradict or overlap, use truth tables or elimination.

---

### 5. **Cause and Effect / Correlation vs Causation**

- Beware of assuming causation from correlation or temporal sequence.  
- To refute causation claims:  
  - Ask if simultaneous or sequential events necessarily imply causation.  
  - Provide alternative causes or confounding factors.  
  - Show that correlation does not equal causation.

---

### 6. **Evaluating Experimental or Survey Data**

- When comparing groups (e.g., test vs control):  
  - Check if baseline conditions are equal (e.g., similar initial scores).  
  - Look for confounding variables (e.g., family background).  
  - Consider if outcome measures fully capture the effect.

- When percentages add up to more than 100%, suspect overlapping categories or multiple responses.

---

### 7. **Logical Form of Arguments**

- Recognize common logical forms:  
  - Modus Ponens: If A then B; A; therefore B.  
  - Modus Tollens: If A then B; not B; therefore not A.  
  - Denying the antecedent or affirming the consequent are invalid.

- Identify when arguments use indirect cause-effect chains to refute claims.

---

### 8. **Handling Ambiguous or Overlapping Relations**

- When family or relational terms overlap, map relations carefully.  
- Count distinct individuals by analyzing possible overlaps.

---

### 9. **Interpreting Quantifiers**

- "All," "some," "none," "at least one," "at most one" have precise meanings.  
- Use Venn diagrams or set logic to analyze statements involving quantifiers.

---

### 10. **Common Logical Fallacies to Watch For**

- **Ignoring necessary conditions:** Assuming a condition is sufficient when it is only necessary.  
- **False dichotomy:** Assuming only two options when more exist.  
- **Hasty generalization:** Drawing conclusions from insufficient data.  
- **Equivocation:** Using ambiguous terms inconsistently.  
- **Confusing correlation with causation.**

---

### 11. **Approach to Complex Multi-Person/Entity Assignments**

- Assign known facts first.  
- Use "if-then" conditions to propagate constraints.  
- Use elimination to narrow down possibilities.  
- Check for consistency with all constraints.

---

### 12. **Evaluating Ethical or Social Arguments**

- Identify the key assumption behind the argument (e.g., advertisers consider ethics).  
- To weaken, show alternative motives or outcomes.  
- To strengthen, show contrasting behavior or consequences supporting the assumption.

---

### 13. **Handling Paradoxical or Contradictory Statements**

- When two premises lead to an absurd conclusion, at least one premise must be false.  
- Use logical deduction to identify which premise is likely false.

---

### 14. **Interpreting Metaphors or Maxims**

- Focus on the implied meaning rather than literal wording.  
- Identify the underlying principle or moral.

---

### 15. **Evaluating Policy or Management Decisions**

- Compare costs and benefits explicitly.  
- Consider whether the action’s cost is justified by the expected benefit.  
- Look for data on effectiveness and efficiency.

---

### 16. **Handling Statements About Necessity and Sufficiency**

- "Only if" indicates necessity.  
- "If" indicates sufficiency.  
- "If and only if" indicates both necessity and sufficiency.

---

### 17. **Dealing with Overlapping Categories in Surveys**

- When percentages exceed 100%, assume respondents can select multiple options.  
- Understand that causes or opinions may be compound, not mutually exclusive.

---

### 18. **Key Tips for Test Situations**

- **Read carefully:** Pay attention to qualifiers like "must," "may," "only," "at least," "at most."  
- **Break down complex sentences:** Translate into logical statements or diagrams.  
- **Check for contradictions:** If conditions conflict, identify which must be false or which element must be excluded.  
- **Use elimination:** Rule out impossible options first.  
- **Look for necessary assumptions:** Identify what must be true for the argument to hold.  
- **Beware of absolute conclusions:** Often, arguments rely on probabilistic or conditional reasoning.
