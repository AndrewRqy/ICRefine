**Pronoun Antecedent Resolution Cheat Sheet for Difficult Cases**

---

### 1. **Ambiguous Pronouns: When Both Referents Are Plausible**

- **Key Insight:** If the sentence structure and context allow the pronoun to logically refer to either of the two possible antecedents, and no additional clues clarify which one, the pronoun is **ambiguous**.

- **Common Patterns:**
  - Both subjects could logically perform the action or possess the attribute described by the pronoun.
  - The pronoun matches both antecedents in number and gender.
  - No contextual or semantic clues favor one antecedent over the other.

- **Examples:**
  - *"Alex did not know Taylor until he sent a letter."*  
    Both Alex and Taylor could have sent the letter → **Ambiguous**
  - *"The nurse stayed away from the laborer because they were very busy."*  
    Both nurse and laborer could be busy → **Ambiguous**
  - *"The developer met with the secretary because they made a mistake."*  
    Both could have made a mistake → **Ambiguous**
  - *"The mechanic greets the receptionist because she was in a good mood."*  
    Both could be in a good mood → **Ambiguous**
  - *"The scientist collaborated with the artist, and he shared a story."*  
    Both could have shared the story → **Ambiguous**

- **How to Identify:**
  - Check if the pronoun’s number and gender fit both antecedents.
  - Consider if the action or attribute can logically apply to both.
  - Look for contextual clues; if none exist, mark as ambiguous.

---

### 2. **Pronouns Referring to the Subject Doing the Action vs. Object**

- When the sentence involves an action directed from subject (X) to object (Y), and the pronoun could refer to either:

  - **If the pronoun is the object of a verb or preposition, it usually refers to the object (Y).**
  - **If the pronoun is the subject of a clause explaining the subject’s state or action, it usually refers to the subject (X).**

- **Examples:**
  - *"The designer showed the cook a blueprint and asked him for feedback."*  
    "Asked him" → likely the cook (object) → **Cook**
  - *"The carpenter collaborated with the designer and gave him a prototype."*  
    "Gave him" → likely the designer (object) → **Designer**
  - *"The cleaner called the guard and asked her to open the door."*  
    "Asked her" → likely the guard (object) → **Guard**

- **Exceptions:**  
  When the pronoun is ambiguous due to lack of context or both roles fit, mark as ambiguous.

---

### 3. **Pronouns in Causal Clauses ("because", "since", "as")**

- When the pronoun appears in a causal clause explaining a reason, consider:

  - **Who logically would have the reason or attribute?**
  - **Is the reason more naturally associated with the subject or the object?**

- **Examples:**
  - *"The surgeon warned the nurse that she needed more time to prepare."*  
    Both surgeon and nurse could need more time → **Ambiguous**
  - *"The lawyer looked into accusations against the cashier because he needed to understand the case."*  
    The lawyer is the one investigating → **Lawyer**
  - *"The sheriff protected the writer because they uphold the peace."*  
    "Upholding the peace" fits sheriff’s role → **Sheriff**
  - *"The cook is always teaching the assistant new techniques because they like to teach others."*  
    Teaching is the cook’s role → **Cook**

- **Tip:**  
  Use real-world knowledge about roles and responsibilities to guide interpretation.

---

### 4. **Possessive Pronouns with Multiple Possible Owners**

- When a possessive pronoun (his, her, their) could refer to either of two antecedents:

  - **Check which antecedent logically owns or is associated with the possessed noun.**
  - **If both are plausible and no context clarifies, mark as ambiguous.**

- **Examples:**
  - *"The customer and the chef discuss her culinary training."*  
    Culinary training is associated with the chef → **Chef**
  - *"The reporter and the chef will discuss their favorite dishes."*  
    Could be both or either → **Ambiguous**
  - *"The supervisor gave the employee feedback on his/her/their stellar performance."*  
    Feedback is about employee’s performance → **Employee**

- **Tip:**  
  Use semantic knowledge about typical ownership or association.

---

### 5. **Pronouns Referring to Groups vs. Individuals**

- Plural pronouns (they, their) can refer to:

  - **Either a group (both individuals together)**
  - **Or one individual if singular they is used**

- **If the sentence involves two people and "they/their" is used:**

  - **If the pronoun logically refers to both together, accept plural group reference.**
  - **If it could refer to either individual alone and no context clarifies, mark as ambiguous.**

- **Example:**
  - *"The scientist collaborates with the artist, and they share a story."*  
    Could mean both together → **Ambiguous** (if options do not include "both")

---

### 6. **Pronouns Referring to People vs. Inanimate Objects**

- Pronouns like "he", "she", "they" usually refer to people or animals.

- **If one antecedent is a person and the other is an object or abstract noun, pronoun usually refers to the person.**

- **Example:**
  - *"The pathologist obtained tissue samples from the victim to look at under her microscope."*  
    Microscope belongs to pathologist (person), not victim → **Pathologist**

---

### 7. **Pronouns with Gender Mismatch**

- Pronouns must agree in gender and number with their antecedents.

- **If a pronoun is singular feminine ("she", "her") and one antecedent is plural or masculine, the pronoun likely refers to the singular feminine antecedent.**

- **Example:**
  - *"After meeting with the producers, Sam goes to her office."*  
    "Her" is singular feminine → likely Sam (if Sam is female), not producers (plural) → **Sam**

---

### 8. **Pronouns Referring to the Speaker or Listener**

- In sentences involving communication verbs ("told", "asked", "disclosed"):

  - The pronoun in the reported clause usually refers to the speaker (subject of main clause), not the listener (object).

- **Example:**
  - *"Alex tells us that he could not meet."*  
    "He" refers to Alex (speaker), not "us" (listeners) → **Alex**

---

### 9. **When Pronouns Refer to the Person Being Addressed**

- In requests or commands involving calling and asking:

  - The pronoun usually refers to the person being called (object), not the caller (subject).

- **Example:**
  - *"The guard called the cleaner and asked her to open the door."*  
    "Her" refers to the cleaner (object), not the guard (subject) → **Cleaner**

---

### 10. **When Pronouns Refer to the Subject’s Own Possessions or Relations**

- Phrases like "my parent and his/her secretary" usually mean the secretary belongs to the parent, not the secretary’s own secretary.

- **Example:**
  - *"My parent called his secretary for more information."*  
    "His" refers to "my parent" → **Parent**

Question: In the following sentences, explain the antecedent of the pronoun (which thing the pronoun refers to), or state that it is ambiguous.
Sentence: The nurse stayed away from the laborer because they were very busy.
Options:
(A) The nurse was busy
(B) The laborer was busy
(C) Ambiguous
Reasoning: Let's think step by step.  
Here we need to determine who the pronoun "they" might be referring to. There are two possible referents for "they": the nurse and the laborer. The sentence is of the form "X stayed away from Y because (X or Y) were very busy."  
Let's consider Y first: "X stayed away from Y because Y was very busy." This makes sense, as X might avoid Y if Y is busy.  
Now, consider X: "X stayed away from Y because X was very busy." This also makes sense, as X might avoid Y if X is busy.  
Because both interpretations are possible and the sentence does not provide enough context to choose between them, the antecedent of "they" is ambiguous. So the answer is (C).
Answer: (C)

Question: In the following sentences, explain the antecedent of the pronoun (which thing the pronoun refers to), or state that it is ambiguous.
Sentence: The patient disclosed to the counselor that they had a history of substance abuse.
Options:
(A) The patient had a history
(B) The counselor had a history
(C) Ambiguous
Reasoning: Let's think step by step.  
Here we need to determine who the pronoun "they" refers to. There are two possible referents: the patient and the counselor. The verb "disclosed" and the phrase "had a history of substance abuse" might help us decide. Let X be the patient and Y the counselor. The sentence is of the form "X disclosed to Y that (X or Y) had a history of substance abuse."

Let's consider Y first: "X disclosed to Y that Y had a history of substance abuse." This would mean the patient is telling the counselor about the counselor's own history, which is unlikely, since the counselor would already know their own history.

Now, consider X: "X disclosed to Y that X had a history of substance abuse." This makes sense, because the patient is sharing personal information with the counselor.

Because in this context, X is the patient and Y is the counselor, the answer should be the patient. So the answer is (A).
Answer: (A)
