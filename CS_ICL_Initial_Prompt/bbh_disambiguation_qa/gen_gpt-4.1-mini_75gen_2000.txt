**Pronoun Antecedent Determination Cheat Sheet for Difficult Cases**

---

### 1. **Ambiguity arises when both possible antecedents are plausible and no clear contextual clues exist.**

- **Typical ambiguous structures:**
  - Sentences of the form:  
    - "X disliked Y because he/she is arrogant."  
    - "X greets Y because he/she was in a good mood."  
    - "X met with Y because they made a mistake."  
    - "X and Y discussed his/her/their favorite dishes."  
    - "X planned to meet Y at her/their office."  
    - "X was running from Y because he/she smelled awful."  
    - "X was unable to communicate with Y because he/she/they used big words."  
    - "X told Y that he/she did something."  
    - "X told Y that he/she/they could do something."  
    - "X asked Y if he/she/they could borrow money."  
    - "X and Y will discuss his/her/their favorite dishes."

- **Key sign of ambiguity:**  
  Both antecedents fit logically and grammatically, and the sentence provides no additional context to favor one over the other.

- **How to handle:**  
  - State **Ambiguous** if no contextual or semantic clues favor one antecedent.  
  - Avoid guessing based on stereotypes unless strongly supported by context.

---

### 2. **Use semantic and real-world knowledge to resolve ambiguity when possible.**

- **Examples of semantic clues:**

  | Sentence Type | Clue | Interpretation |
  |---------------|------|----------------|
  | Referral or expertise (e.g., "The patient was referred to the specialist because she is an expert") | Expertise usually belongs to the specialist | Pronoun refers to specialist |
  | Payment or responsibility (e.g., "X informed Y that she would need to make payments") | Client usually pays, not engineer | Pronoun refers to client |
  | Job roles and typical actions (e.g., "The janitor warned the child... he would have to mop") | Janitor is responsible for mopping | Pronoun refers to janitor |
  | Physical actions or abilities (e.g., "The worker told the pedestrian that he should avoid...") | Advice usually directed to the listener | Pronoun refers to pedestrian |
  | Professional knowledge (e.g., "The customer and the chef discuss her culinary training") | Culinary training belongs to chef | Pronoun refers to chef |
  | Logical cause-effect (e.g., "The developer was unable to communicate with the writer because he focuses on code") | Developers focus on code, writers on writing | Pronoun refers to developer |
  | Gender and number agreement (e.g., "After meeting with the producers, Sam goes to her office") | "Her" singular feminine → Sam (if producers plural) | Pronoun refers to Sam |

- **How to apply:**  
  - Identify roles and typical behaviors of entities.  
  - Use real-world knowledge to eliminate unlikely antecedents.  
  - Check pronoun number and gender agreement with antecedents.

---

### 3. **Pronouns referring to plural antecedents or groups**

- **Plural pronouns ("they," "their") can refer to:**
  - One of the individuals (if singular antecedents are possible)  
  - Both/all individuals collectively (if multiple antecedents mentioned)

- **If sentence structure or pronoun number conflicts with singular antecedents, consider plural or group reference.**

- **Example:**  
  - "The scientist collaborates with the artist, and they share a story."  
    - "They" likely refers to both together (plural).  
    - If options do not include "both," answer is ambiguous.

---

### 4. **Pronouns in sentences with multiple people of the same gender**

- When two people of the same gender are mentioned, and the pronoun is singular (he/she/her/his), ambiguity is common unless context clarifies.

- **Example:**  
  - "The mechanic disliked the cashier because he is arrogant."  
    - Both male; "he" could refer to either.  
    - Without context, answer is ambiguous.

---

### 5. **Pronouns in sentences with possessive pronouns referring to offices, belongings, or abstract concepts**

- When the pronoun refers to possession (e.g., "her office," "their blueprints"), ambiguity arises if both parties could logically own or be associated with the object.

- **Example:**  
  - "Bailey planned to meet the director at her office."  
    - Could be Bailey's or director's office.  
    - Without further context, answer is ambiguous.

---

### 6. **Pronouns referring to people in indirect speech or reported speech**

- When someone tells or asks another person about "he/she/they," consider who would logically be the subject of the reported action.

- **Example:**  
  - "The assistant asked the physician if he could borrow some money."  
    - More likely the assistant is asking if the assistant can borrow money.  
    - If context supports, choose the more logical antecedent.

---

### 7. **Pronouns referring to people in cause-effect or reason clauses**

- When the pronoun is in a clause explaining a cause or reason ("because he/she/they..."), consider who the cause or reason most logically applies to.

- **Example:**  
  - "The developer was unable to communicate with the writer because they focus on code."  
    - Developers focus on code, so "they" likely refers to developer.

- If both could logically be the cause, answer is ambiguous.

---

### 8. **Pronouns referring to people in sentences with verbs of communication or action**

- When the pronoun is the object or subject of verbs like "told," "asked," "warned," "helped," consider who is likely performing or receiving the action.

- **Example:**  
  - "The guard called the cleaner and asked her to open the door."  
    - "Her" likely refers to the cleaner (the one being asked).

---

### 9. **Gender and number agreement is crucial**

- Pronouns must agree in number and gender with their antecedents.

- **Example:**  
  - "After meeting with the producers, Sam goes to her office."  
    - "Her" is singular feminine, "producers" is plural, so "her" refers to Sam.

- If pronoun number/gender does not match any antecedent, answer is ambiguous.

---

### 10. **When in doubt, choose "Ambiguous"**

- If after applying all above reasoning, no clear antecedent emerges, or both are equally plausible, select **Ambiguous**.

---

# Summary Table for Difficult Cases

| Situation | How to Decide | Example |
|-----------|---------------|---------|
| Both antecedents plausible, no context | Ambiguous | "X disliked Y because he is arrogant." |
| Semantic knowledge favors one | Choose logical antecedent | "Patient referred to specialist because she is expert." → Specialist |
| Pronoun number/gender mismatch | Eliminate mismatches | "Sam goes to her office" → "her" = Sam |
| Plural pronouns with multiple people | Could be group or ambiguous | "Scientist and artist, and they share a story." |
| Possessive pronouns with possible multiple owners | Ambiguous unless context | "Meet at her office." |
| Reported speech with logical speaker/listener | Choose logical speaker/listener | "Assistant asked physician if he could borrow money." → Assistant |
| Cause-effect clauses | Choose who logically causes effect | "Unable to communicate because they focus on code." → Developer |
| Communication/action verbs | Pronoun likely refers to person receiving action | "Guard called cleaner and asked her to open door." → Cleaner |
