# CHEAT SHEET: Deductive Validity in Logical Arguments

---

## 1. **Necessary vs. Sufficient Conditions**

- **"A is necessary for B"** means:  
  If B, then A.  
  (B ⇒ A)

- **"A is sufficient for B"** means:  
  If A, then B.  
  (A ⇒ B)

- **Contrapositive:**  
  The contrapositive of "If A, then B" is "If not B, then not A."  
  Both are logically equivalent.

- **Common Error:**  
  Do **not** reverse the direction!  
  - "If A, then B" does **not** mean "If B, then A."
  - "A is sufficient for B" does **not** mean "A is necessary for B."

---

## 2. **Subset and Membership Logic**

- If "All X are Y," then every X is a Y, but not every Y is an X.
- If "All X are Y" and "Z is an X," then "Z is a Y."
- If "All X are Y" and "Z is a Y," you **cannot** conclude "Z is an X."

---

## 3. **Disjunctions ("or") and Negations**

- "Nobody is neither A nor B" means:  
  For every person, at least one of A or B is true.  
  (¬A ∧ ¬B) is false for everyone ⇒ (A ∨ B) is true for everyone.

- "No X is both A and B" means:  
  For all X, at least one of ¬A or ¬B is true.  
  (A ∧ B) is false for all X ⇒ (¬A ∨ ¬B) is true for all X.

---

## 4. **Existential Statements ("Somebody", "There exists")**

- "Some X is Y and Z" means:  
  There is at least one X who is both Y and Z.

- If a property is required for all members of a group, and you find an X who lacks that property, then not all X are in that group.

---

## 5. **Transitive Reasoning**

- If A ⇒ B and B ⇒ C, then A ⇒ C.
- If A is sufficient for B, and B is sufficient for C, then A is sufficient for C.

---

## 6. **Common Invalid Inferences**

- **Affirming the Consequent:**  
  If A ⇒ B, do **not** conclude B ⇒ A.

- **Denying the Antecedent:**  
  If A ⇒ B, do **not** conclude ¬A ⇒ ¬B.

- **Reversing Subset Logic:**  
  If all X are Y, do **not** conclude all Y are X.

- **From "If A, then not B" and "not B," do not conclude A.**

---

## 7. **How to Test Validity Step-by-Step**

1. **Translate** each premise into logical form (using ⇒, ∧, ∨, ¬).
2. **Identify** what the conclusion claims (is it a reversal, contrapositive, or direct application?).
3. **Check for Contrapositives:**  
   If the conclusion is the contrapositive of a premise, it's valid.
4. **Check for Reversal:**  
   If the conclusion reverses the direction of implication, it's invalid.
5. **For subset/membership:**  
   If the conclusion tries to go from "Y" to "X" when only "X ⇒ Y" is given, it's invalid.
6. **For existential statements:**  
   If the premises guarantee the existence of an element with certain properties, check if the conclusion matches.
7. **For "or"/"and" statements:**  
   Remember De Morgan's Laws:
   - ¬(A ∨ B) ≡ (¬A ∧ ¬B)
   - ¬(A ∧ B) ≡ (¬A ∨ ¬B)

---

## 8. **Quick Reference Table**

| Statement Type | Valid Conclusion | Invalid Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| "A is necessary for B" | B ⇒ A | A ⇒ B |
| "A is sufficient for B" | A ⇒ B | B ⇒ A |
| "All X are Y" | X ⇒ Y | Y ⇒ X |
| "No X is Y" | X ⇒ ¬Y | ¬Y ⇒ X |
| "Some X is Y" | ∃X(Y) | ∀X(Y) |
| "If not A, then not B" | B ⇒ A (contrapositive) | A ⇒ B (invalid) |

---

## 9. **Special Patterns**

- **If the conclusion is a restatement or contrapositive of a premise, it's valid.**
- **If the conclusion requires a reversal of implication, it's invalid.**
- **If the conclusion assumes a subset relation in the wrong direction, it's invalid.**
- **If the conclusion assumes that a property is both necessary and sufficient when only one is given, it's invalid.**

---

## 10. **Practice: Spot the Error**

- If the argument concludes "All Y are X" from "All X are Y" → **INVALID**
- If the argument concludes "If not B, then not A" from "If A, then B" → **VALID** (contrapositive)
- If the argument concludes "If A, then B" from "If not B, then not A" → **VALID** (contrapositive)
- If the argument concludes "If B, then A" from "If A, then B" → **INVALID**

Is the argument, given the explicitly stated premises, deductively valid or invalid?
Options:
- valid 
- invalid
Reasoning: Let's think step by step.  
The first premise says "no member of Juventus is an expert of Getafe CF," so being a member of Juventus rules out being an expert of Getafe CF. The second premise says "somebody is a follower of West Ham United FC and an expert of Getafe CF," so there exists at least one person who is both a follower of West Ham United FC and an expert of Getafe CF. If every follower of West Ham United FC were a member of Juventus, then this person would also be a member of Juventus, but that would contradict the first premise (since members of Juventus cannot be experts of Getafe CF). Therefore, not every follower of West Ham United FC is a member of Juventus. The conclusion follows necessarily. So the answer is valid.

We may conclude: Being an occasional purchaser of Clearly Natural soap is necessary for being an occasional purchaser of Kirk's soap."
Is the argument, given the explicitly stated premises, deductively valid or invalid?

- valid 
- invalid

The third premise says that every occasional purchaser of Kirk's soap is either a frequent consumer of Bath & Body Works soap or a rare consumer of Nioxin shampoo.
