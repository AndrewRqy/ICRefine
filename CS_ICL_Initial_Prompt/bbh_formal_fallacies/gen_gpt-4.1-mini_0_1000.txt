**Cheat Sheet for Deductive Validity of Logical Arguments**

---

### Key Logical Concepts and Patterns

1. **Sufficient and Necessary Conditions**

   - **"A is sufficient for B"** means: If A, then B.  
     Symbolically: A → B  
     *You cannot infer B → A from this.*

   - **"A is necessary for B"** means: If not A, then not B.  
     Symbolically: B → A  
     *You cannot infer A → B from this.*

   - **Common mistake:**  
     - Inferring the converse (B → A) from A → B is invalid.  
     - Inferring the inverse (¬A → ¬B) from A → B is invalid unless explicitly stated.

2. **Contraposition**

   - From A → B, you can validly infer ¬B → ¬A.  
   - This is often used to validate arguments involving necessary conditions.

3. **Transitivity of Implication**

   - If A → B and B → C, then A → C.  
   - This is valid and often used to chain premises.

4. **Disjunctions (OR statements)**

   - From A → (B or C), you cannot conclude A → B or A → C individually.  
   - From (¬B or ¬C) and A → (B or C), you may sometimes infer A → B or A → C depending on premises.

5. **Negations and Double Negations**

   - ¬(¬A) = A  
   - Be careful with statements like "Nobody is neither A nor B" which means "Everyone is A or B."

6. **Existential vs Universal Quantifiers**

   - "Some" or "There exists" statements do not allow universal conclusions.  
   - "Every" or "All" statements allow universal conclusions.

7. **De Morgan’s Laws**

   - ¬(A and B) = ¬A or ¬B  
   - ¬(A or B) = ¬A and ¬B

---

### Common Difficult Patterns and How to Handle Them

#### A. **Mistaking Necessary for Sufficient (and vice versa)**

- Example:  
  Premise: "Being a cousin of Chris is sufficient for not being a son of Kermit." (Cousin → ¬Son)  
  Conclusion: "Whoever is not a son of Kermit is a cousin of Chris." (¬Son → Cousin)  
  This is invalid because the converse does not hold.

- **Tip:**  
  Always check if the conclusion reverses the direction of implication. If yes, it’s invalid unless contraposition or equivalence is explicitly stated.

#### B. **Invalid Converse and Inverse Inferences**

- From A → B, invalid to infer B → A or ¬A → ¬B.  
- Valid to infer ¬B → ¬A (contrapositive).

- **Tip:**  
  When conclusion flips the direction of implication, mark it invalid unless contraposition is used.

#### C. **Disjunctions and Negations**

- Premise: "Every ingredient of X is not an ingredient of Y or not an ingredient of Z."  
- Conclusion: "Every ingredient of X is an ingredient of Y or an ingredient of Z."  
- This is invalid because the premise states a disjunction of negations, not a disjunction of affirmations.

- **Tip:**  
  Carefully translate disjunctions and negations; do not flip "not (A or B)" into "not A and not B" without proper justification.

#### D. **Existential Statements and Universal Conclusions**

- Premise: "Some A is B."  
- Conclusion: "All A are B."  
- Invalid.

- **Tip:**  
  Existential premises cannot support universal conclusions.

#### E. **Using Transitivity Correctly**

- If premises chain implications properly (A → B and B → C), then A → C is valid.

- **Tip:**  
  Look for chains of implications to validate conclusions.

#### F. **Handling "Nobody is neither A nor B"**

- This means: "Everyone is A or B."  
- From this, you can infer necessary conditions about membership.

- **Tip:**  
  Translate carefully and use De Morgan’s laws to understand the meaning.

#### G. **Negation of Conjunctions and Disjunctions**

- From "No one is both A and B," you can infer "If A then not B" and vice versa.

- **Tip:**  
  Use this to infer necessary conditions.

### Step-by-Step Approach to Evaluate Validity

1. **Identify the type of statements:**

   - Are they universal (all, every) or existential (some, there exists)?  
   - Are they conditional (if-then), necessary, or sufficient?

2. **Translate premises into logical form:**

   - Use symbols: A, B, C for properties or relations.  
   - Express premises as implications, conjunctions, disjunctions, or negations.

3. **Check the conclusion:**

   - Does it follow directly from premises?  
   - Does it reverse implication direction? (Watch for invalid converse)  
   - Does it generalize existential to universal? (Invalid)  
   - Does it use contraposition correctly?

4. **Look for transitive chains:**

   - If premises chain A → B and B → C, then A → C is valid.

5. **Analyze disjunctions carefully:**

   - From A → (B or C), you cannot infer A → B or A → C alone.  
   - From ¬(B and C), infer A → (¬B or ¬C).

6. **Use De Morgan’s laws for negations:**

   - ¬(A or B) = ¬A and ¬B  
   - ¬(A and B) = ¬A or ¬B

7. **Avoid invalid inferences:**

   - Do not infer converse or inverse without justification.  
   - Do not infer universal from existential.

---

### Examples of Valid Inferences (Patterns to Recognize)

- **Transitive implication:**  
  If A → B and B → C, then A → C.

- **Contraposition:**  
  From A → B, infer ¬B → ¬A.

- **From "No A is B" and "All C are A," infer "No C is B."**

- **From "Nobody is neither A nor B," infer "Everyone is A or B."**

- **From "Being A is necessary for B," infer "If not A, then not B."**

---

### Examples of Invalid Inferences (Patterns to Avoid)

- Inferring converse: From A → B, concluding B → A.

- Inferring inverse: From A → B, concluding ¬A → ¬B.

- Inferring universal from existential: From "Some A is B," concluding "All A are B."

- Inferring affirmation from negated disjunctions without proper logic.

### Tips for Test Time

- **Translate carefully:** Put premises and conclusions into "if-then" form.

- **Check direction of implications:** Does conclusion reverse the direction? If yes, likely invalid.

- **Look for contraposition:** Can you get the conclusion by contraposition?

- **Beware of "some" vs "all":** Existential premises do not support universal conclusions.

- **Disjunctions:** Do not split "or" statements into separate affirmations without justification.

- **Use transitivity:** Chain implications to reach conclusions.

- **Negations:** Apply De Morgan’s laws carefully.

- **If stuck, test with counterexamples:** Imagine a case where premises are true but conclusion false.
