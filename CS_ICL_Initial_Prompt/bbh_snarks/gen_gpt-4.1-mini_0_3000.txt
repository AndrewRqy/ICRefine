**Cheat Sheet for Identifying Sarcasm in Challenging Examples**

---

### Key Indicators of Sarcasm

1. **Contradiction Between Statement and Reality**
   - The statement says the opposite of what is true or expected.
   - Example: Praising something obviously bad or criticizing something obviously good.
   - Look for exaggerated praise or blame that conflicts with common sense or facts.

2. **Exaggeration or Hyperbole**
   - Extreme overstatements that are clearly not meant literally.
   - Example: Claiming a $50,000 scholarship is “poor” or a $10 per mile fuel cost is “reasonable.”

3. **Irony Through Mismatched Labels or Descriptions**
   - Positive labels applied to negative actions or vice versa.
   - Example: Calling someone “a brilliant person” for doing something harmful or “the best” after describing laziness.

4. **Mocking or Ridiculing Absurdity**
   - Statements that mock unrealistic or absurd ideas by pretending to accept them.
   - Example: Suggesting “banning breathing” or “kids paying for school maintenance.”

5. **Rhetorical Questions or Statements That Pretend Ignorance**
   - Asking questions or making statements that imply the opposite is obvious.
   - Example: “How could government control of the internet be bad?” (implying it obviously is bad).

6. **Use of Positive Language to Describe Negative Situations**
   - Calling a negative event “the best,” “great,” or “fun” to highlight the absurdity.
   - Example: “A wave of hypothermia and drownings will be a great way to start the year.”

7. **Incongruity Between Cause and Effect**
   - Linking unrelated or illogical cause and effect to mock reasoning.
   - Example: “He’s over six feet, so he must be wonderful.”

8. **Exaggerated Simplification of Complex Issues**
   - Oversimplifying serious problems to mock naive or dismissive attitudes.
   - Example: “Hey just be happy then you won’t be depressed anymore.”

### Tips for Applying This Cheat Sheet

- **Focus on the mismatch between literal meaning and context:** If the statement’s literal meaning conflicts with common sense, reality, or the context, it’s likely sarcastic.

- **Look for exaggerated praise or blame:** Sarcasm often uses over-the-top language to mock or criticize.

- **Check if the statement mocks a stereotype or naive belief:** Sarcasm often targets oversimplifications or prejudices.

- **Identify rhetorical devices:** Sarcasm frequently uses rhetorical questions or ironic statements to imply the opposite.

- **Beware of positive words describing negative situations:** Words like “great,” “best,” “amazing,” or “fun” used in obviously negative contexts are strong sarcasm signals.

- **Consider the tone implied by the statement:** Even if the words seem positive, the tone (mocking, ironic) can reveal sarcasm.

Question: Which statement is sarcastic?
Options:
(A) You mean Knuth up arrow notation isn't unfamiliar for you?
(B) You mean Knuth up arrow notation isn't normal for you?
Reasoning: Let's think step by step.  
We need to identify which statement is sarcastic. Option (A) says "You mean Knuth up arrow notation isn't unfamiliar for you?" — this suggests surprise that someone is actually familiar with a very obscure mathematical notation, which is a sincere expression of surprise. Option (B) says "You mean Knuth up arrow notation isn't normal for you?" — this implies that it is normal to be familiar with a highly specialized notation, which is an unreasonable expectation and thus sarcastic. The sarcasm comes from pretending that something obscure is actually common knowledge. So the answer is (B).
Answer: (B)
